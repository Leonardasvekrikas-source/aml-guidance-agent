# aml-guidance-agent

[![CI](https://github.com/Leonardasvekrikas-source/aml-guidance-agent/actions/workflows/ci.yml/badge.svg)](https://github.com/Leonardasvekrikas-source/aml-guidance-agent/actions/workflows/ci.yml)

A retrieval-augmented agent that answers questions about AML typologies and
regulatory expectations, grounded in public guidance documents — and refuses when
the corpus doesn't support an answer.

Built to be run by a stranger, and actually tested that way: the repository was
cloned into a fresh directory and driven from this README alone. That test found
three real defects, all logged in
[`findings/what-broke.md`](findings/what-broke.md).

> **Status.** Every number below has been measured. The retrieval benchmark and
> the full 40-question answer evaluation have both been run end to end.
>
> One thing is still outstanding: the groundedness figure comes from an LLM
> judge whose agreement with a human has **not yet been measured**.
> `results/judge_agreement.md` holds 20 sampled decisions awaiting grading, and
> until that is done groundedness should be read as a judge's opinion rather
> than a validated metric. The rest — citation validity, refusal accuracy,
> answer rate, cost and latency — are computed in code and need no judge.
>
> Nothing in this README is claimed that a committed script cannot reproduce.

---

## Why this exists

Most retrieval systems are evaluated on whether they return something plausible.
In a regulated domain that is the wrong bar — a fluent answer citing a regulation
that doesn't say what the answer claims is worse than no answer at all.

This project takes the opposite approach:

1. **The retrieval layer is benchmarked, not assumed.** Lexical, dense and hybrid
   retrieval are compared on a hand-authored evaluation set with known correct
   passages.
2. **Citations are verified in code, not by the model.** Every claim the agent
   makes must trace to text that was actually retrieved. Unverifiable claims are
   rejected before the answer is returned.
3. **The evaluation set contains questions the corpus cannot answer.** A system
   that scores well on answerable questions and bluffs on the rest has not been
   evaluated.

### Reranking, and where the bottleneck actually is

A cross-encoder (`bge-reranker-v2-m3`) reranks the top 50 hybrid candidates.
Exact gold on `t480`:

| | Recall@5 | Recall@10 | MRR | Median latency |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Hybrid RRF | 0.767 | 0.800 | 0.694 | 48 ms |
| **Hybrid + rerank** | **0.883** | **0.911** | 0.733 | 683 ms |

A 14x latency cost, which is why reranking sits behind the first stage rather
than replacing it — a cross-encoder over all 9,910 chunks would take minutes
per query.

**The candidate sweep** ([`results/candidate_sweep.json`](results/candidate_sweep.json),
`make eval-sweep`) reports the *ceiling* — what fraction of gold passages
reached the candidate pool at all, the best the reranker could possibly do —
against what it achieved:

| Candidates | Ceiling | Achieved@5 | Headroom used | Median latency |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 10 | 0.833 | 0.833 | 100% | 187 ms |
| 25 | 0.883 | 0.850 | 96.2% | 375 ms |
| **50** | 0.917 | **0.883** | 96.4% | 687 ms |
| 100 | 0.950 | 0.850 | 89.5% | 1136 ms |
| 200 | 0.950 | 0.850 | 89.5% | 1134 ms |

Two things worth reading off this. The ceiling keeps climbing to 0.950 at 100
candidates, so the first stage does eventually find almost everything. But the
reranker stops keeping up: it delivers 100% of available headroom at 10
candidates and only 89.5% at 100, and **achieved recall actually falls** beyond
50. More candidates means more plausible distractors to discriminate against,
and past a point the reranker loses more to confusion than the wider pool
gains.

So 50 is not a default, it is the measured optimum, and the bottleneck is now
shared rather than sitting entirely in the first stage.

### Chunk size, and two measurement artifacts that hid the answer

Three profiles, page-level gold (the only definition valid across chunk sizes):

| Profile | Avg tokens | Avg pages covered | Rerank R@5 | Rerank R@10 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| `t256` | 256 | 1.47 | 0.794 | 0.861 |
| **`t480`** | 478 | 1.89 | **0.811** | **0.911** |
| `t1024` | 1014 | 2.86 | 0.639 | 0.767 |

`t480` wins. But the first version of this table read `t1024` at 0.483, and
that number was wrong twice over:

**Artifact one — a chunk was credited to the page it started on.** A
1024-token chunk averages ~4,900 characters against ~2,700 characters per page,
so it typically spans two to three pages. Page-level scoring asks whether
retrieval returned a chunk from the page the answer is on; under start-page
attribution, a large chunk that *literally contained the answer* scored as a
miss whenever the answer fell on its second page. Chunks now record a page
span (`migrations/003`), and `t1024` moved 0.483 → 0.606.

**Artifact two — the reranker was truncating.** The cross-encoder window was
hardcoded to 512 tokens, so a 1024-token chunk was scored on its opening half
and the rest was invisible. No error, just a worse ranking that looked like the
chunk size being bad. `t1024` moved 0.606 → 0.717 once the window fitted it.

Only after removing both is the conclusion trustworthy: **larger chunks really
are worse here**, but by far less than the raw numbers first suggested, and for
a real reason — dilution — rather than two bugs in the measurement.

The reranker now warns when its window is smaller than the passages it is
scoring, because that failure is otherwise completely silent.

### What the retrieval numbers actually show

Full per-question breakdown in [`results/retrieval_analysis.md`](results/retrieval_analysis.md),
regenerated by `make eval-analysis`.

**Hybrid wins on average, and loses the best case.** On 5 of the 30 questions,
RRF ranks the gold passage *worse* than the better of its two inputs. The
clearest case is `a16` (shell-company red flags): BM25 returns the gold passage
at rank 1, dense does not return it in the top ten at all, and fusion drops it
to rank 6 — because RRF promotes chunks dense ranked highly, and dense was
wrong on that question. Fusion buys robustness across question types by giving
up the best case on any single one. That trade is worth making when an LLM
reads the whole top-k rather than only the first result, but it is a real cost
and it is not usually reported.

**Neither retriever dominates, and which one leads depends on chunk size.** On
`t480` with exact gold, BM25 beats dense on recall@5 (0.650 vs 0.600). At
`t256` the ranking flips on page-level recall@5 (0.733 dense vs 0.650 lexical).
"Dense is better" would have been the wrong conclusion from either profile
alone.

**Where BM25 wins, it wins on rare exact tokens.** Questions carrying
distinctive regulatory vocabulary — `a16`, `a23` (underground banking cash-for-
value swaps) — are pinned almost entirely by high-IDF terms. An embedding
places such a term near its semantic neighbours, which is the wrong behaviour
when those neighbours are other regulatory jargon about something else.

**Where dense wins, the question avoids the document's vocabulary.** Around
half the evaluation questions were written deliberately in an analyst's own
words rather than the source phrasing. On `a09` and `a05`, BM25 scores 0.000
and 0.125 while dense scores 1.000 — there is simply nothing for lexical
matching to catch.

### Answer quality (M4)

| Metric | Value |
|---|---|
| Groundedness — claims supported by retrieved text | 0.731 |
| Citation validity — cited passage was actually retrieved | 1.000 |
| Refusal accuracy on unanswerable questions | 0.900 |
| Answer rate on answerable questions | 0.867 |
| Median latency | 101.3 s |
| Median retrieval calls per question | 9 |
| Median cost per question | $0.446 |

Over 30 answerable and 10 unanswerable questions; 26 answers were judged for groundedness.

Answer rate is reported next to groundedness deliberately. A system that refuses every question scores perfect groundedness and is useless; the two numbers only mean something together — and this project has the receipt: an earlier run scored groundedness 0.842 at an answer rate of 0.633, and fixing the cause of the missing answers moved groundedness *down* to 0.731 while making the system strictly more useful.

Drafting runs on Claude Opus 5; the citation-support check and the groundedness judge run on Sonnet, since both are narrow one-claim classifiers rather than open-ended reasoning. Prompt caching in the agent loop cut uncached input per question from 85,286 tokens to 103.

## Architecture

```
question
   │
   ▼
┌─────────────────────────────────────────────┐
│  agent loop (hand-written, no framework)    │
│  • decides whether to search again          │
│  • reformulates on weak results             │
│  • hard cap on iterations                   │
│  • may return "insufficient evidence"       │
└──────────────┬──────────────────────────────┘
               │  search(query, k)
               ▼
┌─────────────────────────────────────────────┐
│  hybrid retrieval                           │
│  BM25 ∪ pgvector, fused with RRF            │
└──────────────┬──────────────────────────────┘
               │  chunks + provenance
               ▼
┌─────────────────────────────────────────────┐
│  drafting                                   │
│  answer written only from retrieved chunks  │
└──────────────┬──────────────────────────────┘
               │
               ▼
┌─────────────────────────────────────────────┐
│  validation  ← the point of the project     │
│  every citation checked against retrieved   │
│  text; unsupported claims reject the draft  │
└──────────────┬──────────────────────────────┘
               │
               ▼
      answer + citations, or refusal
```

**No agent framework.** The loop is a few hundred lines of plain Python. This is
deliberate: LangChain or LlamaIndex would hide the two things this project is
actually about — when the loop decides to stop, and what happens to a claim that
fails validation. Both are explicit here and can be read top to bottom.

## Stack

| Layer | Choice | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Store | PostgreSQL 16 + pgvector | One database for lexical metadata, chunks and embeddings. No second service to run. |
| Lexical retrieval | `rank_bm25` over the chunk set | Real BM25 on a corpus small enough to hold in memory. Honest to describe as BM25, unlike Postgres `ts_rank`. |
| Dense retrieval | `bge-base-en-v1.5` via sentence-transformers | Runs locally on the RTX 4090. No API cost per experiment, so re-running the benchmark is free. |
| Fusion | Reciprocal Rank Fusion | Standard, no tuning, no training. |
| Generation | Anthropic API | Reliability over local for the drafting step; the embedding side is where local matters. |
| Service | FastAPI | One `/ask` endpoint. |
| Deployment | Docker Compose | App + Postgres. `docker compose up` is the whole setup. |

## Corpus

Public regulatory and typology guidance only. Every document is recorded in
`corpus/manifest.yaml` with source URL, publisher, publication date and retrieval
date. Nothing is committed that isn't publicly downloadable.

Target: 20–30 documents drawn from FATF typology and guidance reports, EBA ML/TF
risk factor guidelines, FinCEN advisories, Wolfsberg Group papers and Europol
threat assessments.

## Running it

Needs Docker. An `ANTHROPIC_API_KEY` is required for the agent, validation and
the judge; **retrieval and the M1 benchmark run without one.**

```bash
git clone https://github.com/Leonardasvekrikas-source/aml-guidance-agent
cd aml-guidance-agent
cp .env.example .env          # add ANTHROPIC_API_KEY for M2-M4

make up                       # Postgres + pgvector, schema from migration
make ingest                   # download 30 documents, chunk, embed, load
make eval-retrieval           # M1 - no API key needed
make eval-answers             # M4 - needs a key
make report                   # rewrite the tables above from results/
make serve                    # API on :8000
```

**On Windows**, `make` is not installed by default. `run.ps1` mirrors every
target with the same names:

```powershell
.
un.ps1 up
.
un.ps1 ingest
.
un.ps1 eval-retrieval
```

**With an NVIDIA GPU**, ingestion embeds ~8,500 chunks in seconds rather than
minutes:

```bash
docker compose -f docker-compose.yml -f docker-compose.gpu.yml up -d
```

The GPU reservation lives in a separate override file on purpose — putting it
in the base compose file makes `docker compose up` fail outright on a machine
with no NVIDIA runtime.

### Asking a question

```bash
make ask Q="What indicators suggest trade-based money laundering in invoice documentation?"
```

```bash
curl -X POST localhost:8000/ask   -H 'content-type: application/json'   -d '{"question": "What indicators suggest trade-based money laundering in invoice documentation?"}'
```

The response carries a `trace_id`. `GET /traces/{id}` returns the full run,
**including drafts that validation rejected** — a citation-validating system
that cannot show you a rejection has not demonstrated anything.

## Limitations

Written honestly, and kept current.

**The judge is unaudited.** Groundedness comes from an LLM judge whose
agreement with a human has not been measured. `results/judge_agreement.md`
holds 20 sampled decisions awaiting grading; until `make judge-audit-score` has
run, that 0.731 is a second model's opinion with a decimal point on it. Every
other figure in the answer table is computed in code.

**Contextual retrieval is implemented but not run.** The code and its cost
estimator are committed; the API budget ran out first. First-stage recall is
the measured bottleneck, so this is the most likely next improvement — and it
is currently a claim about what should help, not a result.

**The evaluation set has a single author.** It reflects one view of what a
correct answer looks like. Inter-annotator agreement is unmeasured, and with 30
answerable questions the confidence interval on any recall figure is wide —
a difference of one or two questions moves recall@5 by three points.

**Groundedness will be scored by an LLM judge.** An unaudited judge is a second
model's opinion with a decimal point on it. `make judge-audit` samples its
decisions for human grading and reports the disagreement rate; that number is
the error bar on groundedness and belongs in the repo whether or not it is
flattering.

**Validation cannot catch several real failure modes**, documented in full in
`src/aml_agent/validation/validator.py`. Briefly: a claim supported by its
cited chunk but misleading in context; errors of omission; two individually
supported claims that together imply something neither supports; and a claim
faithfully citing a guidance document that is itself out of date.

**Retrieval figures carry two error bars, both larger than they look.** With 30
answerable questions, one question moves recall@5 by 3.3 points, so differences
smaller than that are noise. Separately, HNSW is an approximate index whose
graph depends on insertion order, so dense and hybrid figures wobble by roughly
±0.02 at the tail between index builds — verified by rebuilding from a clean
clone, where exact-gold figures reproduced to the digit and two page-level
dense numbers moved slightly. Neither error bar is small enough to support a
claim that one retriever beats another by a point or two.

**Page-level and exact gold are not comparable.** They are separate tables for
that reason. Page-level figures are systematically higher because a page can
hold several chunks and any of them counts.

**The corpus is guidance, not case law or enforcement actions.** Questions
about specific enforcement outcomes are out of scope by construction — several
of the ten unanswerable questions are precisely that shape.

**English only.** All 30 documents are English. A Spanish-language GAFILAT
typology report was dropped from the manifest during sourcing for that reason.

**Chunk boundaries lose context.** An answer split across a chunk boundary is
retrievable only in part, and the `t256` / `t480` comparison measures the
symptom without fixing it. No reranker and no query expansion.

**This is a portfolio system, not a compliance tool.** It has no place in a
real reporting workflow.
